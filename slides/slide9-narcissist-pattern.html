Nine A — recognising the pattern

Are you dealing with a narcissist?

Recognition, not diagnosis — only a clinician diagnoses a personality disorder.

The core

Grandiosity, a constant need for admiration, and absent empathy — pervasive, and lifelong. Everything below is only how that shows up on an ordinary day.

Two faces, one engine

  • Grandiose / overt. Loud, dominating, visibly self-important. Easy to spot, so it gets all the attention.
  • Covert / vulnerable. Quiet, wounded, the perpetual victim. Harder to name, because it arrives asking for your sympathy.

Only the volume differs. The entitlement, and the missing empathy, are identical.

Under criticism — the most revealing thirty seconds

“I’m sorry you feel that way.”

Non-apology · grammatically an apology, functionally a verdict on you

“After everything I’ve done for you, this is what you bring up?”

DARVO · deny, attack, and arrive as the injured party

Three days of silence, then normality — as if nothing was ever raised.

Narcissistic injury · rage or ice. Reflection is the one thing that never comes.

In conversation

  • You mention a hard week; two sentences later they are describing a harder one. Yours never comes back.
  • “You ran 5K? I did a half-marathon on a sprained ankle.”
  • No genuine curiosity — questions only gather material to talk about themselves.

In relationships

  • Week one: “I have never felt this before.” Week six: cold, unexplained — and you are the one chasing.
  • You say “I felt hurt when…” and somehow end the conversation apologising.
  • They read your feelings perfectly and still do the thing. Cognitive empathy, not affective — it was never a misunderstanding.
  • Scorekeeping: the dinner they paid for months ago exists; your ten things do not.

At work — the waiter test

  • Team ships — “I drove this.” Project slips — “my junior dropped the ball.”
  • Charming upward, dismissive downward. How someone treats a person who cannot help them is the most honest information you will ever get.
  • They take apart a colleague who isn’t in the room — which is the sound of you, after you leave it.

Over years

  • A trail of “crazy” exes, “toxic” bosses, “backstabbing” friends — and never one word of their own part in it.

The four-part cycle — and it loops

ILove bombing. Overwhelming affection and future-planning at the start. Builds dependency, lowers your guard.

IIDevaluation. Warmth withdraws; criticism and coldness replace it. You start performing to get phase one back.

IIIDiscard. Abrupt exit or shutdown once you stop being useful — often once new supply is secured.

IVHoovering. Pulling you back after the discard: crisis calls, apologies, nostalgia, “I’ve changed.” Then back to I.

Gaslighting — denying facts until you doubt your own memory.

Triangulation — “even they agree with me.”

Future faking — promises that buy compliance now, never delivered.

Moving the goalposts — the standard shifts the moment you meet it.

Silent treatment — punishment by withdrawal until you cave.

Smear campaign — your reputation rewritten before you can speak.

Word salad — circular conflict, exhausting by design. You give up; that was the win.

Projection — accusing you of precisely what they are doing.

Nine B — checking it, and what to do

Check it against yourself first.

They can manage their presentation. They cannot manage your nervous system.

The feeling test — more reliable than any trait list

  • You feel anxious before seeing them, and drained afterwards.
  • You rehearse sentences before saying them, and edit yourself constantly.
  • You increasingly doubt your own memory of events.
  • You over-explain and over-apologise — including for things that were never yours.
  • You leave conversations confused, and cannot reconstruct why.
  • Your world has quietly shrunk: fewer friends, less family, hobbies dropped.
  • You have started keeping screenshots and notes, just to stay sane.
  • The good moments feel like relief rather than joy.
  • People who know you say you seem different lately.

Three or more — act on it. Whatever the label turns out to be, and whether or not anyone is ever diagnosed. The label was never the thing you needed. Your footing was.

What to do — boundaries are things you do, not things you declare

  • Stop trying to win it. Insight is the missing part; you cannot supply it from outside. Chasing the admission is what keeps people hooked for years.
  • Act, don’t announce. “I won’t discuss this while you’re shouting” — and then actually leaving the room.
  • Starve the reaction. For contact you cannot avoid, grey rock: calm tone, short factual answers, no emotion, no detail. Caveat: under-studied, and it can escalate cyclically abusive situations.
  • Write it down. Dated notes and saved messages — for your own mind first, and for any HR or legal question later.
  • Rebuild what got eroded. Isolation is the mechanism, not a side effect.
  • Plan the hoover now, while you are calm — not at one in the morning, when you are lonely.
  • Get your own support. Avoid couples counselling as a first step if there is active abuse — joint sessions get weaponised.

What this is not — the word is badly over-used

Confidence and ambition. Healthy self-esteem is stable; grandiosity is brittle and needs an audience.

A terrible year. Grief, burnout, depression and addiction all produce real self-absorption — a bad season, not a personality structure.

Neurodivergence. Autistic and ADHD traits get mislabelled constantly. The difference is intent: not perceiving your feelings is not the same as perceiving them and using them.

One account of a conflict — including yours. Abusers accuse others of being narcissists. DARVO looks exactly like this from the inside.
